It is eleven at night, and Nora is watching her clothes spin.
The laundromat hums.
A green backpack sits between her feet, the same one she carried through college.
Her phone lies face up on the folding table.
No messages.
Not one.
She checks it anyway.
Three weeks ago, she moved to a city where she knows nobody.
Two weeks ago, the job that was supposed to justify the move disappeared.
Tonight, the loudest voice in her life is a dryer.
Most of us picture a certain kind of person falling into a cult.
Gullible.
Troubled.
Easy to fool.
But what if that picture is the first lie?
What if the only thing a cult truly needs is a night exactly like this one?
In a few minutes, Nora will say yes to something small.
Over the next eleven months, she will pass through seven stages, one after another.
Each will feel gentle.
Each will feel reasonable.
And somewhere along the way, a stranger far outside her sight will begin pulling on a single loose thread.

Here is the idea this story is built on.
A cult does not seize a mind.
It builds a room, one comfortable wall at a time, and waits for a person to walk into it on their own.
Is that really so unusual?
Singer spent decades working with former members, and she described recruits as people who were often lonely, between attachments, and sometimes mildly depressed.
A Education Week report on her work added something surprising.
A researcher quoted there said that people who join cults are generally not the children of troubled families.
They were people in transition.
People between things.
People like Nora.
